# Outlook: what comes next, what the Holocene allows, and the volume ledger

**Kīlauea, written 2026-07-13, during the pause before episode 51.** Synthesis of
three research sweeps (`notes/future_scenarios.md`, `notes/holocene_record.md`,
`notes/volume_ledger.md`).

## 1. What potentially comes next

HVO's published scenario list, in their stated order of likelihood:

1. **Episodes continue** as long as supply/vent equilibrium holds. Currently ~11-day
   average cadence; no signs of ending per HVO (Dec 2025), supply metrics steady
   (annualized output ~0.18 km³/yr — *above* Puʻuʻōʻō's long-term ~0.12 km³/yr).
2. **Vent breakdown → sustained effusion.** The named precedent path: Puʻuʻōʻō's 47
   fountaining episodes gave way in 1986 to continuous effusion (Kupaianaha);
   Mauna Ulu 1969's 12 episodes did the same. HVO: "The vents could break down at
   some point and begin to erupt lava more continuously."
3. **New vent near Halemaʻumaʻu or in the upper SWRZ.** Episode 30 (Aug 2025) already
   demonstrated the mechanism in miniature — a shallow dike opening a new fissure
   through the south crater wall with hours of precursory rim seismicity. HVO's named
   analog for the bigger version: 1919–20, when a long-lived Halemaʻumaʻu eruption
   spawned the 9-month Maunaiki eruption on the SWRZ.
4. **Migration to the ERZ — "least likely"** per HVO given current deformation, BUT
   the ERZ is demonstrably re-pressurizing post-2018: the June–July 2024 upper-ERZ
   intrusion (>1,700 quakes, Pauahi–Maunaulu), the Sept 2024 Nāpau eruption ("magma
   intruding from the summit into the East Rift Zone"), and ongoing inflation of the
   Makaopuhi–Nāpau storage region. The Kīlauea Iki precedent (summit fountains →
   ERZ eruption at Kapoho within weeks, 1960) is the historical template. Volcano
   Watch also flags new **pit-crater formation** as a non-rare outcome (≥17 times in
   550 years) and notes intrusions can trigger south-flank earthquakes.

**The Mauna Loa wildcard:** Pietruszka et al. 2024 (J. Petrology) show from ~200
years of lava chemistry that a shared mantle source alternates between the two
volcanoes on decadal timescales, and that since ~2010 melt has been diverting toward
Mauna Loa "for the first time since the mid-20th century" (consistent with its 2022
eruption). Deformation studies show anticorrelated, stress-coupled behavior. If that
chemical trend is real and continues, the current Kīlauea eruption may be running on
a supply allocation with a clock on it — an unreconciled tension in the literature,
and arguably the strongest published support for a "show winds down in years, not
decades" view.

**Long-term (centuries):** HVO's own framing ("The past is the key to the future"):
eventually supply drops drastically, the caldera collapses deep enough to intersect
the water table, and a multi-century explosive period resumes. Today's configuration
(high supply, refilling caldera) is the effusive end of that cycle.

## 2. What the last few thousand years allow

The Swanson et al. (2014) cycle, the master context: the past 2,500 years divide into
3 effusive and 2 explosive multi-century periods; the explosive mode held ~60% of the
time; supply during explosive centuries was ~1–2% of effusive-era rates; explosivity
correlates with a caldera floor at/below the water table.

Styles in the repertoire that could return, with their configurations:

- **ʻAilāʻau-type decades-long flow** (~1410–1470): ~60 years of continuous tube-fed
  summit effusion, 5.2 ± 0.8 km³, covered ~430 km² of Puna, dug Kazumura Cave. The
  scaled-up version of "vents break down → sustained effusion." Puna has been
  inundated only ~3 times in 5,000 years (intervals ~1,250–2,200 yr).
- **Caldera collapse** (~1470–1510, and 2018 in miniature): prolonged reservoir
  drainage → roof failure. 2018 proved it takes withdrawal of <4% of stored magma to
  start (Anderson et al. 2019). The ~1500 collapse birthed the present Kaluapele.
- **Keanakākoʻi-type explosive centuries** (~1500–1820): phreatic/phreatomagmatic
  explosions from a deep caldera intersecting the water table; includes the 1790
  pyroclastic surge (deadliest US volcanic event). Requires BOTH supply collapse and
  a caldera ~600 m deeper than today's refilled floor — the 2019–2020 water lake was
  the modern demonstration that 2018 briefly restored that geometry before refilling
  reversed it. Not the current trajectory, but the eventual future per HVO.
- **High-fountaining summit episodes — the current mode — are in the record too:**
  tephra studies (Volcano Watch, Apr 2026) show high fountains within Kaluapele
  ~1500, ~1650, and in the first two decades of the 1800s (the "golden pumice");
  "the current eruption style is not unprecedented." Modern instances: 1959 Kīlauea
  Iki (supply pulse ~5× long-term rate), Mauna Ulu onset 1969, Puʻuʻōʻō onset
  1983–86, now 2024–26. It is the volcano's signature move when fresh, gas-rich,
  high-rate supply meets a constricted young vent.
- **19th-century-style continuous lava lake** (1823–1924): a century of near-continuous
  summit lake (~3 km³ erupted into the caldera by 1840), punctuated by drainings,
  ending in the 1924 groundwater steam-explosion crisis. The 2008–2018 Overlook lake
  was a modern echo; further refill makes a persistent lake configuration plausible
  again.
- **SWRZ activation:** rare but recurrent — undocumented weeks-to-months eruptions in
  the 1790s–1800s, the 1823 Keaīwa flow from the Great Crack, 1868, Maunaiki 1919–20,
  1971, 1974, June 2024. Historically clusters at regime transitions and summit
  high-stands — both boxes arguably ticked now.

Headlines from the ledger: the 2018 summer moved as much magma in three months as
Puʻuʻōʻō typically moved in 7–10 years; everything erupted at the summit since 2020
combined (~0.47 km³) is still only ~60% of that one summer; and the current episodic
eruption, unremarkable-looking next to those numbers, is actually running at the
highest sustained effusion *rate* since at least the early Puʻuʻōʻō era — direct
evidence the deep supply remains high, and the main argument against an imminent
wind-down.
